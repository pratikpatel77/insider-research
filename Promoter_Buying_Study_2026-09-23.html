Promoter Buying Study

Can publicly disclosed buying by promoters, their relatives and other insiders be traded on NSE? This study tests every insider filing NSE published from November 2015 to today against what the shares did next, after costs.

The answer in brief

"A promoter bought shares" is not a reliable signal on its own. Across 8,268 liquid promoter-side market purchases, the stock beat comparable stocks by +2.7% on average over the next 120 sessions. But the median stock lagged by -2.2%, and only 46% of them beat their peers. Counting each company once a year, the edge becomes statistically zero (t = 0.9). The averages are propped up by a few companies whose promoters buy nearly every week. For example, Adani Ports filed 206 buys and Chambal Fertilisers 198.

One kind of insider buy does carry information: an individual (a promoter as a person, a relative, a director or senior manager) using their own money to raise their personal holding by at least 5%, in a company worth ₹10,000 crore or less, where insiders have not been selling.

Across 340 such signals since 2016 (about 32 a year), holding each stock for 120 sessions beat matched peers by +15.3% a year after costs (t = 4.9). It worked in both halves of the sample: +14.0% a year in 2016–21 and +16.8% a year in 2022–26. The same purchase made by a promoter's holding company or trust showed no edge (-1.4% a year).

What this means for you
  • Use the rules below to build a watchlist, not a buy list. The edge is real but modest: the median signal beats its peers by only about 2% over six months, and the gains come from a minority of big winners. Your own entry and exit setups and position sizing do the rest.
  • The effect builds over 3–12 months, not days. The first 20 sessions add little. This fits positional holding in cash, not short swing trades.
  • Hold many signals (10–15 at a time). With a 53% hit rate and wide dispersion (the worst tenth fell -29% against peers and the best tenth rose +53%), a handful of positions is mostly luck.
  • About 40% of the measured excess shows up even on random dates in the same stocks. So part of the edge is "the kind of company where insiders buy", and the part that comes from the timing of the buy is closer to +9% a year. That is still worthwhile. Plan around the lower number.

The rules

Run these after market hours each day. About 70% of filings arrive after 4 pm; the backtest always enters at the next day's open, even for filings made during market hours. A stock that qualifies stays on the watchlist for 120 trading sessions from the day it was disclosed. The rules were fixed before looking at the current list below.

  1. Genuine open-market purchase. The filing type is Buy, the mode is Market Purchase, and the security is equity. Everything else is ignored: inter-se transfers, gifts, ESOPs, preferential allotments, warrant conversions, off-market deals, pledge creations and releases, and scheme allotments. 8,235 promoter "buys" in the data were one of these.
  2. An individual insider is buying. The buyer is a promoter, promoter-group member, relative, director or KMP who is a person. Holding companies, LLPs, family trusts, investment firms, funds and employee-welfare/ESOP trusts do not count. Their buying is usually about control or consolidation, and it showed no edge.
  3. Conviction: raises their own holding by at least 5%. Shares bought ÷ shares that person held before the purchase ≥ 5%. This relative size mattered; rupee size did not. Buys above ₹25 crore had no edge, often because big buyers were holding companies.
  4. Company worth ₹10,000 crore or less. The sweet spot was ₹1,000–5,000 crore. Large caps showed no edge.
  5. Tradeable. EQ series (not trade-to-trade or SME), median daily traded value ≥ ₹1 crore over 60 sessions, traded on at least 95% of days, no more than 2 circuit hits in the last 20 sessions, price ≥ ₹10.
  6. No insider selling. No promoter-side market sale in the prior 180 days (for director/KMP signals, no insider sale in the prior 90 days). This filter matters: in 2022–26 the "bought but also sold" group lost -24% a year against peers.
  7. Watch for 120 sessions. Enter with your own setup. A new qualifying buy restarts the 120-session clock. Treat a later insider sale as a reason to exit.
What did not help. These add-ons were tested and did not improve results reliably across both periods:
  • requiring a 50- or 200-day moving-average confirmation before entry
  • only taking signals when the market is above its 200-DMA
  • requiring several insiders to buy
  • requiring a first-ever buy
  • requiring a minimum % of company bought
  • adding up the last 90 days of buying
  • a points-based "priority score"
Keep the rules simple. Every extra filter here was more likely to be fitting noise.

Portfolio and risk

  • Diversify. A 10-position version (equal money per slot, first-come first-served, 120-session holding) returned 30.6% a year from 2016 with a -66% worst drawdown. From 2022 it returned 36.2% a year with a -25% drawdown. Nifty 500 returned 12.1% a year from 2016 (-38% drawdown) and Smallcap 250 15.5% (-61%).
  • Expect big drawdowns in small-cap bear markets. The signal picks stocks relative to their peers; it does not protect you when all small caps fall. In 2018 and 2019 the signal portfolio fell further than the market. Your stop-loss and sizing rules are the protection.
  • A 20% stop-loss from entry slightly improved the 10-position results in the simulation (see the backtest table). Treat it as a reasonable default rather than a proven improvement.

Your questions, answered

Which insider purchases have been more meaningful?

These carried information:

  • Purchases by people rather than holding companies.
  • Purchases that are large relative to what that person already owns.
  • Purchases in small and mid-sized companies, especially ₹1,000–5,000 crore.
  • Purchases with no insider selling alongside.

Director and KMP purchases that meet the same tests work as well as promoter purchases (+17.6% a year, 114 signals). Relatives of promoters (spouses, children, siblings) showed some of the strongest results, but the sample is small.

Does sizable promoter buying precede positive performance?

For the average buy, only a little. For the buys that pass the rules, yes, over the following 3–12 months. The size that matters is the stake size relative to the insider's own holding, not the rupee amount. Very large rupee purchases (above ₹25 crore) showed no edge, because they are usually holding-company or creeping-acquisition purchases made for control.

How do I tell a meaningful buy from a routine one?

Red flags, in order of how much they mattered:

  • The buyer is a company, LLP or trust.
  • Insiders have also been selling.
  • It is a large cap.
  • The purchase is a tiny addition to a large existing holding.
  • It isn't a market purchase at all (inter-se, gift, ESOP, preferential, off-market, pledge-related).

Two further checks catch bad data:

  • A filed price that doesn't match the day's trading range means the filing is wrong. 32,974 promoter market-buy rows contained 544 such errors, usually a value typed in lakhs.
  • A filing made more than 120 days after the trade is stale.

What minimum size is worth considering?

  • Use +5% of the insider's own holding. Thresholds of 2%, 10% and 20% also worked. 2% was a bit weaker, and 10–20% gave slightly higher returns but fewer signals.
  • Every filing already exceeds ₹10 lakh, since SEBI only requires disclosure above that level.
  • Buys under ₹25 lakh were weaker when looked at signal by signal, but adding a ₹25 lakh floor did not improve the 2016–21 portfolio results. It appears as a note on the current list, not as a rule.
  • "% of company bought" gave no consistent edge.

How should multiple purchases be combined?

  • Treat them as one position. Each new qualifying buy restarts the 120-session window.
  • Adding up 90 days of buying did not beat the simpler single-buy test.
  • "Several insiders buying at once" was not reliable. Four or more buyers in 90 days actually did worse, often because it was a coordinated family consolidation.
  • Promoter and director signals in the same stock add up.
  • A later insider sale cancels the signal.

What time horizon makes sense?

+1.6% at 20 sessions, +4.5% at 60, +8.6% at 120 and +18.2% at 250 sessions (averages versus peers). 120 sessions (about six months) gave the best balance of return and reliability. Holding 180–250 sessions earned a similar amount in total but less per year of capital used.

Sectors, company sizes, market conditions

Size: ₹2,000–5,000 crore was strongest, ₹5,000–10,000 crore weak, and above ₹10,000 crore nothing. Market conditions: the signal worked in rising and falling markets. It was most dependable when the Nifty 500 was more than 10% below its peak (median +6.7% versus peers over 120 sessions). Insiders buying into weakness is where their information shows most. Sectors: there were too few signals per sector to rank them honestly. Only 36% of signal companies could be mapped to a current NSE industry, and no sector except capital goods had 15 or more signals. Treat any sector story as unproven.

Risks, false signals and limits

  • Small-cap beta. Signals sit in ₹500–10,000 crore companies. In the 2018–20 small-cap bear market the portfolio fell about two-thirds from its peak. The edge is relative, so it will not stop a crash.
  • Lopsided outcomes. A typical signal does little. A minority of large winners carries the average. Few positions, early exits on noise or chasing only the "best-looking" names can all lose the edge.
  • Why insiders buy isn't always information. Promoters also buy to support the price when shares are pledged, before fund-raising, to meet takeover-code limits or to defend control. The individual-buyer and no-selling rules remove much of this, but not all.
  • How the rules were found. About 30 variants were tried. The split between people and entities was spotted while looking at the full sample, so 2022–26 is a consistency check rather than a pristine out-of-sample test. The core result (t ≈ 4.9, positive in both halves and in 9 of 11 years) is well above the stricter t ≥ 3 bar used for many-tests research, but future returns will probably be lower than the backtest.
  • Data. Company filings contain typing errors. They are checked against traded prices and corrected or dropped here, but some will slip through. NSE changed its insider-filing system in May 2026, and the tool reads both formats. Prices are adjusted with NSE's corporate-action list plus a rule for missing splits and bonuses. A genuine single-day crash above 30% in an F&O stock could occasionally be misread as a split.
  • Only NSE-listed companies. BSE-only companies were not studied. Most are illiquid and would fail the tradeability rule anyway.
  • Costs and capacity. Costs assume delivery trades at retail size. Positions above about 1–2% of a stock's daily traded value will face more impact cost than modelled.
  • Crowding. Insider-buying screens are now common. The 2022–26 results are still strong, but the edge could shrink as more people act on the same filings.

How the study was done

Data

  • Insider filings: every NSE insider-trading disclosure under SEBI (PIT) Regulations, 2015, Reg. 7(2), from 19 Nov 2015 to 23 Sep 2026. That is 332,565 rows after removing duplicates, read from NSE's older JSON feed up to May 2026 and from the per-filing XBRL files after that.
  • Prices: NSE daily price files for every security, including stocks later delisted, so failed companies are not left out. Split and bonus adjustments come from NSE's corporate-action history. Daily circuit-hit files and NSE index closes were also used.

Timing

A trade counts from the first session after NSE broadcast the filing, entering at that day's open. A filing broadcast before 9 am counts from the same day. The median gap from the insider's trade to disclosure was 4 days.

Benchmark

Each stock is compared with an equal-weight portfolio of stocks in the same liquidity quintile and 6-month-momentum tercile, which gives 15 peer groups rebuilt daily. That removes the plain "small caps did well" effect. Nifty 500 and Smallcap 250 are shown for reference.

Costs

STT of 0.1% each side, stamp duty, exchange, SEBI and GST charges come to about 0.25% round trip. Slippage is added on top: 0.05–0.5% per side, higher for less liquid stocks.

Statistics

Event averages use monthly averages with Newey–West t-statistics that allow for overlapping holding periods. Portfolio results use daily returns. 2016–21 was used to shape the rules and 2022–26 to check them (see the caveat under Risks).

Staying within the rules

  • These disclosures are published by NSE under SEBI's PIT Regulations. Trading on published information is legal.
  • Do not use this if you are a designated person, connected person or relative of an insider of a company on the list, or if you hold any unpublished price-sensitive information (UPSI) about it. Your own company's trading-window and pre-clearance rules still apply to you.
  • Never act on insider "tips" ahead of a filing. That is exactly what the regulations prohibit.
  • This is research, not investment advice. It was not prepared by a SEBI-registered investment adviser or research analyst. Past results do not guarantee future returns.
